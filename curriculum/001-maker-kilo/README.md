# Maker Realm (Kilo) — Ages 8+

Students deepen their understanding of programming without yet moving into text-based coding. At this stage, ByteBear Academy shifts from “robot play” to “inventor mindset”: students still work on highly engaging projects, but they are increasingly expected to test ideas, work in teams, explain their thinking and improve designs through iteration. The four-student pod model becomes a more visible instructional tool here as students divide roles, compare ideas and prepare for pod-based build showdowns. This is also the stage where the **BBC micro:bit** joins the LEGO Education platform as a second, individually owned device, which makes the realm workable online as well as in a classroom. Python is first introduced in the next stage, Innovator.

## micro:bit as the Online and Take-Home Platform

The LEGO Education kits assume a shared physical kit and a pod seated around one table, which does not transfer cleanly to a remote class. At the Maker realm the **BBC micro:bit** supplements LEGO as the low-cost device each student can own outright. It carries its own sensors, LED display, buttons, speaker and radio, it is programmed in the same block-based style through **Microsoft MakeCode**, and MakeCode's built-in simulator lets a student write, run and debug a working program in the browser even before hardware arrives or when a device is left at school.

The two platforms carry the same concepts—events, loops, variables, conditionals and sensor input—so students move between them without relearning the programming model. That lets a single curriculum serve both delivery formats:

- **In person:** LEGO remains the primary build platform for pod work; micro:bit is the individual device used for practice, homework and data collection between sessions, so every student codes on their own hardware rather than waiting for a turn at the shared kit.
- **Online:** micro:bit becomes the primary hands-on device, shipped to each learner as a small starter pack (micro:bit v2, battery pack and USB cable — nothing else). LEGO models appear as instructor-led demonstrations on camera, which preserves the build narrative without requiring families to buy a kit. Add-ons are **not** part of the starter pack: the rover kit belongs to Quest `01`, the radio pack to Quest `10` and the inventor's pack to Quest `11`, each bought or shipped only when a family books that quest.

The micro:bit also keeps the realm's collaboration model intact online. Students working in the same room can use its radio to link devices directly; remote pods instead agree a shared message format and each build one part of a distributed system, which turns the constraint of being apart into a genuine mission about interfaces and protocols.

## Maker Quests

Strands 5 to 8 are the concepts; the quests below are how they are sequenced, taught and booked. Quest `00` runs on the micro:bit and nothing else. Quest `01` is the rover quest, and its missions are built around a robot that has to move through a real room.

| Quest | Name | Hardware required | Concept strands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **`00`** | Signals | micro:bit v2, battery pack, USB cable — no add-on | Strands 5 and 6: events, variables, loops, conditionals, functions, debugging, sensors and data |
| **`01`** | Motion | Quest `00` hardware **plus the rover kit (required add-on)** | Strand 7: actuators, control loops, proportional control, simple machine learning |
| **`10`** | Messages | Quest `00` hardware **plus the radio pack (required add-on)**: a second micro:bit v2 with its own battery pack, and a 24‑LED ZIP halo | Strand 8 first half: radio, addressing, message protocols, one system split across several devices |
| **`11`** | Machines | Quest `00` hardware **plus the inventor's pack (required add-on)**: edge connector and breadboard, two servos, a gripper or arm bracket, external sensors, buzzer, LEDs and crocodile clips | Strand 8 capstone: a pod-designed machine that is nobody's kit, documented and presented |

Each quest after `00` names one required add-on, and each one changes what a student can physically make. The add-ons are small, individually affordable and cumulative — a student who finishes the realm owns a micro:bit, a rover, a second micro:bit, a light ring and a box of parts, all of which keep working after the course ends and all of which reappear in the Quest `11` build.

- **Quest `01` — the rover kit.** The micro:bit stops being something on a desk and becomes something that crosses a room.
- **Quest `10` — the radio pack.** A second micro:bit means a student has a real two-device network in their own room, so radio is taught properly online rather than simulated: one device sends, the other answers, and a dropped or misread message is something they can see happen. The ZIP halo is what makes the invisible part visible — every packet that arrives lights a colour, so a protocol the pod invented becomes a thing you can watch running.
- **Quest `11` — the inventor's pack.** Servos, a gripper, external sensors and a breadboard, with no instructions for any particular model. This is the first quest where the hardware does not tell the student what to build: pods design a machine, and the rover and radio pack from the earlier quests are theirs to bolt onto it.

Quests run in order within a realm. By Quest `11` a student owns everything the earlier quests required, and the capstone assumes it: the rover and the radio pack are parts on the table alongside the inventor's pack. A student who joins the realm partway through either buys the earlier add-on or borrows a loaner for the missions that need it, which is the same arrangement used when a kit has not arrived in time.

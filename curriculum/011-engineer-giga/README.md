# Engineer Realm (Giga) — Ages 14+

This stage marks the transition away from LEGO-based systems and single-purpose microcontrollers and onto the **Raspberry Pi** running **Linux** and standard **CPython**. Learners stop programming a chip and start administering a computer: a filesystem, a package manager, users and permissions, a network stack, background services and version control. That shift is what makes real computer vision and AI work possible, because the full Python ecosystem — OpenCV, NumPy, PyTorch and the rest — is now available rather than a cut-down runtime. Because they already built low-voltage circuits on the ESP32 at the Innovator realm, the electronics are familiar; the new ground here is the operating system, the toolchain and the scale of what can run on it. By this stage, the pod structure closely mirrors real engineering collaboration, with teams defending design decisions and competing on performance and execution.

## Hardware at This Stage, and the Cloud Alternative

The Engineer realm is the first point in the program where families buy equipment themselves. Everything earlier is either provided, shared or software-only; here each student needs their own computer to administer, and that computer is theirs to keep and use well beyond the course.

**The recommended purchase** is a Raspberry Pi with 4GB of RAM or more, an official power supply, storage, a case and a camera module or USB webcam. Students can work headless over SSH or VNC from the tablet or laptop they already have, so a separate monitor and keyboard are useful but not required. Sensors and actuators carry over from the Innovator kit, and keeping an ESP32 on the network alongside the Pi is what turns the two into a layered system rather than two unrelated devices. ByteBear should publish a single specified parts list with current pricing and at least two suppliers per region, so families are not left assembling a bill of materials on their own.

**If buying hardware is not an option**, the realm runs on a rented Linux instance instead. A modest cloud VM from any major provider gives the student the same shell, the same CPython, the same OpenCV and the same git workflow, reached through a browser, so a Chromebook or a school-managed laptop is enough. The instance is spun down between sessions and costs a small amount per hour of use.

Two things genuinely differ on the cloud path, and the curriculum should be explicit about them rather than pretending otherwise:

- **No GPIO and no camera.** Strand 15's vision work runs on supplied video files and image datasets rather than a live feed, which is a fair substitute for building and evaluating a pipeline, though it removes the experience of pointing a camera at the world and watching a model fail on it.
- **No physical robot to hand.** Strand 14's GPIO exercises and the Strand 17 capstone need hardware. ByteBear should host a small bank of instructor-managed Raspberry Pis that remote students can book and reach over SSH, with a webcam pointed at the device so a student can watch their own code move a real motor. Cloud-path capstones are then judged on the same system running on shared hardware.

Accounts and billing for rented compute stay with ByteBear rather than the family: instructor-created accounts, spending caps and auto-shutdown on idle instances, with no student holding raw cloud credentials. This keeps a surprise bill impossible and gives instructors a way to reset a broken environment in minutes.

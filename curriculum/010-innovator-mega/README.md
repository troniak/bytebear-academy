# Innovator Realm (Mega) — Ages 11+

At this stage learners combine physical computing with advanced programming. They gain proficiency in **Python** and begin exploring more advanced computational thinking, AI workflows and systems design while still working primarily on LEGO Education computer science and AI kits. This is also where the **ESP32** enters the program, programmed in **MicroPython**, giving remote and at-home learners a real microcontroller of their own at the same moment they start writing text-based code. ByteBear’s differentiator here is that older students are no longer just completing projects, they begin to think like young engineers: comparing tradeoffs, documenting decisions, evaluating model bias and improving the performance of increasingly ambitious robotic systems. The pod model also becomes more rigorous, with students using tablets to coordinate work, review evidence and justify why one build is more effective than another.

## ESP32 and MicroPython for Remote Learning

The device introduced at this stage is the **ESP32**, programmed in **MicroPython**. It is the natural successor to the micro:bit for this age group and the reason the realm works as well at a kitchen table as in a classroom.

- **The language is the realm's language.** Innovator is where students move to text-based Python, and MicroPython is Python. A student writes the same syntax on their laptop and on the board, so learning the hardware costs them no new language — only a new set of constraints.
- **It is genuinely inexpensive and individually owned.** An ESP32 dev board with a breadboard, jumper wires and a handful of sensors is a modest per-student kit that can be shipped once and kept. There is no shared kit to wait for, and a family is not left with an expensive object if the child moves on.
- **Wi-Fi solves the remote collaboration problem.** The micro:bit's radio only reaches across a room. The ESP32 has Wi-Fi on board, so learners in different homes can publish readings to one shared class dashboard, trigger each other's devices and run a joint experiment that would otherwise require everyone to be in the same building. Being remote becomes the subject matter rather than an obstacle.
- **It moves students to the hardware layer without leaving Python.** Real pins, real voltages, real timing: students meet digital and analog input, PWM, pull-ups, power budgets and the gap between what the code says and what the circuit does. That groundwork is what lets the Engineer realm move straight to Linux and computer vision, treating the electronics as known and spending its time on the operating system instead.

Safety and supervision are part of the strand design: work stays at low voltage from USB or a battery pack, never mains; kits ship with pre-stripped wires and clear polarity markings; and the first at-home session is an instructor-led setup walkthrough covering wiring checks, what a hot component means and when to unplug. Instructors should confirm each learner's board is flashed and communicating before any circuit work begins.

## Innovator Quests

Strands 9 to 13 are the concepts; the quests below are how they are sequenced, taught and booked. Quest `00` runs on the starter kit and nothing else, so a family can begin the realm without buying an add-on. Each quest after it names exactly one, stated at booking, and the four run in order as a single arc.

The arc has a shape worth saying out loud, because it is what the realm is *about*. Quest `00` is a bare board on a breadboard: this is what is actually inside. Quest `01` hands the student the same silicon in a case with a screen, a battery and a socket on the bottom: this is what it looks like once somebody has packaged it for a person to use. Quest `10` sends that package out into the world to gather evidence, and Quest `11` gives it eyes and a head that turns. A student who does all four has taken a component they wired by hand and ended up with a machine that recognises things — and can explain every layer between the two, because they built them in order.

Maker's add-ons were things to wire up: a rover, a second micro:bit, a box of servos and clips. Innovator deliberately does not sell the same things again. Its add-ons come from the modular ecosystems that professional and hobbyist embedded work actually uses — a cased device with a screen and a battery, sensor modules that click onto a bus, and a camera with a neural network processor behind it. The student already owns a rover from the Maker realm; this realm gives them a product, an instrument and a pair of eyes instead.

They are also priced to match. Each add-on is specified to cost about what the Quest `00` starter kit costs, so a family's outlay is roughly flat across the realm rather than rising toward a capstone they cannot afford, and a family who stops after any quest has spent a predictable amount. Where a part would break that rule it is specified out: the environment pack carries an inexpensive gas sensor, and mission `011` calibrates it against outdoor air rather than against a £50 reference sensor nobody has to buy.

The add-ons are small, individually affordable and cumulative, and one connector ties them together. The Stick bought at Quest `01` has a Grove socket on the bottom and a pin header on its back; the sensors at Quest `10` and the camera and servos at Quest `11` plug into one or the other, so each purchase makes the previous one worth more rather than replacing it. A student who finishes the realm owns a breadboard kit, a cased handheld computer, a set of environmental sensors and a camera that runs a model they trained — and all of it still works after the course ends.

**One device per session.** From Quest `01` onward the Stick is the only thing a student brings to class and the only thing that needs charging. The ESP32 has no battery and never travels: it lives on a breadboard, and in Quest `10` it becomes a fixture wired up once and left plugged into a wall at home for a week. At no point does a family have to have two devices prepared, charged and packed before a session.

**Nothing in the realm is physically shared.** Every mission runs on hardware one student owns, in one student's home, because a realm that promises at-home learning cannot have a mission that needs everybody in the same room or a single instrument passed around. Where a mission needs something in common, the common thing is never an object: it is a *condition* (outdoor air at an agreed minute, in mission `011`), a *service* (the instructor-hosted broker and dashboard), an *agreement* (the message format the class negotiates), or a *file* (the trained models pods swap in Quest `11`). Files, conditions, services and agreements all travel to sixteen houses. Hardware does not.

Nothing is retired, either. Each quest puts the earlier purchases back to work in a new role rather than superseding them:

| **`00`** | — | The ESP32, on USB power |

- **Quest `01` — the M5 pack.** The jump from a working circuit to a device somebody would carry. Nothing is wired: the engineering moves to laying out a screen that can be read in a glance, structuring a program as states, putting several devices on two shared wires, and making 250 mAh last a day.
- **Quest `10` — the environment pack.** The same four sensors in every student's home, one agreed message format, and a week of readings pooled into a single dataset. The sensors are cheap and disagree with each other, which is why mission `011` calibrates every one of them against a single accurate instrument the class owns between them. Being in different houses is the point — the class dataset has a bedroom that is never aired and a flat on a main road in it, and mission `110` trains a model that meets a home it was never shown.
- **Quest `11` — the vision pack.** A camera with a neural network processor on board, so the model runs on the module rather than on a laptop or in a cloud. Students train their own classes in the browser, read the detections into MicroPython, close a servo loop on what the camera sees, and hand the result to another pod to break.

**Each quest ends by handing over the next one.** Mission `111` is a showcase with families watching, and the last thing that happens in it is not the demonstration: it is the next quest's hardware being opened in the room. The student who has just presented an instrument with nothing to measure is given four sensors; the class that has just named who its own dataset leaves out is given a camera. The box is the add-on the family has already booked, so nothing about it is a surprise purchase — what changes is *when* it is opened, and that it opens attached to the demonstration that earned it. For at-home learners the pack is posted to arrive before the showcase and is asked to stay sealed until the last minutes of the session, so the class opens together on camera; in person the instructor hands it across. Either way nobody opens alone. Quest `11` has no box: the realm ends by pointing at the Raspberry Pi the Engineer realm runs on, and the student takes home the machine they built instead.

**Instructor note:** hand over only to students who are continuing, and where a class is split, run the reveal as a preview rather than a distribution. The moment should never become a visible line between the families who renewed and the families who did not.

Quests run in order within a realm. By Quest `11` a student owns everything the earlier quests required, and the capstone assumes it. A student who joins partway through either buys the earlier add-on or borrows a loaner for the missions that need it.

Costs are indicative UK retail including VAT at the time of writing, for one student buying one of each at list price; a class buying in quantity pays less. The specification is the band and the role, not the price or the part number. Hardware model numbers in this realm move faster than the curriculum does — the Stick named here replaced a model that went end-of-life during its first year. Treat the named parts as the current instance of a role: a cased ESP32 device with a screen and a Grove port, a Grove environmental set, a Grove vision module with on-device inference.

**Before Quest `00` there is a free session.** [Recon: What's On This Board?](../../workshops/esp32/Recon%20-%20Whats%20On%20This%20Board.md) is a 55-minute open workshop that runs for enrolled students and prospective families at the same time. Visitors need only a browser: they meet a simulated ESP32 with three unlabelled sensors on it and have to work out what each one is, then make it react. Enrolled students arrive with their Quest `00` kit sealed and open it live at 0:37, wire a circuit on camera, and run the same program on real hardware — where the threshold everybody chose in the simulator turns out to be wrong, because a simulator is tidy and a bedroom is not. That gap is the argument for the realm, made by the students rather than by a slide. It is also where the realm's unlock chain starts: Recon is the session that opens Quest `00`'s box, exactly as mission `111` of each quest opens the next one's.
